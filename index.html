A brief history of the United States

An unfinished republic

The United States was shaped by bold ideals, contested ground, and generations of people asking who gets to belong.

John Trumbull’s 1819 painting depicts the committee presenting a draft of the Declaration of Independence to Congress, not the signing itself.

Before 1776

A continent of nations

Long before European colonization, North America was home to diverse Indigenous peoples and nations, with distinct governments, languages, trade networks, and relationships to the land. Their histories did not begin with European arrival, and they continue today.

European empires later colonized the continent, bringing waves of migration and conquest. The colonies that became the United States grew through Indigenous dispossession and the forced labor of enslaved Africans, realities that sit at the center of the nation’s history.

Turning points & tensions

A nation takes shape

These milestones offer one path through a much larger story. Each date marks change, not a neat ending.

1607
Colonial America

Jamestown is established

English settlers found Jamestown in Tsenacommacah, the homeland of the Powhatan peoples. It becomes England’s first permanent settlement in the region that is now the United States. In 1619, enslaved Africans are forcibly brought to Virginia, marking an early chapter in slavery in English North America.

1776
Independence

A declaration of ideals

On July 4, the Continental Congress adopts the Declaration of Independence, asserting that “all men are created equal.” The new nation’s claim to liberty coexists with slavery, the exclusion of women from political rights, and the continued seizure of Indigenous lands.

1787
A new framework

The Constitution is written

Delegates draft a Constitution that creates a federal government with separate branches. Ratified in 1788, it establishes a durable framework, while compromises protect slavery and leave voting rights largely to the states.

1861–1865
Civil War & emancipation

The Union fractures, slavery is abolished

Southern states secede and war breaks out over secession and slavery. The Emancipation Proclamation takes effect in 1863, and the Union victory leads to the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolishes slavery, except as punishment for a crime.

1865–1877
Reconstruction

Freedom meets fierce resistance

Reconstruction brings citizenship and equal-protection guarantees through the Fourteenth Amendment, and voting protections for Black men through the Fifteenth. Black communities build schools, churches, and political institutions; white supremacist violence and discriminatory laws undermine those gains.

1920
Expanding the vote

The Nineteenth Amendment is ratified

The amendment bars denying the vote on the basis of sex. It does not secure equal access for all women: racist restrictions continue to disenfranchise many Black, Indigenous, Asian American, and Latina voters.

1954–1965
Civil rights movement

A movement challenges segregation

Years of organizing, protest, and litigation help bring landmark change. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits segregation in public accommodations and employment discrimination; the Voting Rights Act of 1965 targets barriers that denied Black citizens the vote.

1969
Science & society

Apollo 11 reaches the Moon

NASA’s Apollo 11 mission lands astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the Moon. The achievement draws on the work of thousands, including mathematician Katherine Johnson and other Black women whose contributions were long overlooked.

2008
A changing electorate

Barack Obama is elected president

Obama becomes the first Black American elected president. His election is a historic milestone, not an end to racial inequality or disagreement over the country’s direction.

2020–today
The present

History is still being made

A pandemic, renewed movements for racial justice, political polarization, and debates over democracy and rights mark a period of rapid change. The choices made by people across the country continue to shape what the nation becomes.

Looking back, looking ahead

The story is not settled.

American history is not a straight line from founding ideals to fulfillment. It is a continuing argument over power, freedom, and responsibility, carried forward by people whose stories belong in the record. Learning that history means holding achievement and injustice in view at the same time.
